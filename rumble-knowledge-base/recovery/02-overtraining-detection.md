# Overtraining Detection and Prevention

## Overview

Overtraining exists on a spectrum from normal training fatigue to debilitating syndrome. Early detection prevents progression from recoverable overreaching to long-term overtraining syndrome. Prevention is vastly superior to treatment.

## The Overtraining Continuum

Based on the ECSS/ACSM Joint Consensus Statement (Meeusen et al., 2013):

### Functional Overreaching (FOR)
- **What:** Short-term fatigue from intentionally increased training load
- **Duration:** Days (resolves in 1-2 weeks with rest)
- **Performance:** Temporarily decreased, then supercompensates above baseline
- **Treatment:** Planned recovery (deload week)
- **Is this bad?** No — this is how training works. Overload → recover → adapt.

### Non-Functional Overreaching (NFOR)
- **What:** Unplanned, excessive fatigue from training load exceeding recovery capacity
- **Duration:** Weeks (requires 2-4+ weeks to resolve)
- **Performance:** Decreased with slow return to baseline
- **Treatment:** Extended rest, reduced training, nutritional support
- **Is this bad?** Yes — this wastes training time and risks progression to OTS.

### Overtraining Syndrome (OTS)
- **What:** Severe, chronic maladaptation. Multi-system breakdown.
- **Duration:** Months (2-6+ months to resolve)
- **Performance:** Significantly decreased, may not return to previous level
- **Treatment:** Complete rest, medical supervision, nutritional and psychological support
- **Is this bad?** Dangerous. Can end careers. Must be prevented.

## Warning Signs — What to Monitor

### Training Data Signals
- **CTL ramp rate >7 points/week** for 2+ consecutive weeks
- **TSB below -25** for 3+ consecutive days
- **RPE consistently 2+ points above prescribed** for 3+ sessions
- **Training monotony >2.0** (Foster's formula: mean daily load ÷ standard deviation)
- **Unable to complete prescribed intervals** — power drops significantly before end of set
- **Heart rate decoupling >5%** during steady-state efforts (HR drifts up while power stays constant)

### Subjective Signs (Self-Report)
Athletes should be asked about these regularly. Saw et al. (2016) showed subjective measures are better predictors of overtraining than objective biomarkers.

- Persistent muscle soreness that doesn't resolve with warmup
- Sleep disturbances (difficulty falling asleep, waking frequently, unrefreshing sleep)
- Elevated resting heart rate (+5 bpm above baseline)
- Loss of motivation / dread of training
- Irritability, mood changes, anxiety
- Appetite loss or unexplained weight changes
- Increased susceptibility to illness (frequent colds)
- Declining performance despite consistent training
- Heavy or "dead" legs at the start of every session

### Red Flags — Immediate Action Required
If 3+ of the above are present simultaneously, STOP increasing training load.

If the following are present, recommend medical consultation:
- Performance decline lasting >2 weeks despite rest
- Persistent symptoms of illness
- Signs of hormonal disruption (amenorrhea in women, loss of libido)
- Symptoms consistent with RED-S / low energy availability

## Prevention Strategies

### Structured Load Management
- **Ramp rate:** Increase CTL by no more than 5-7 points per week
- **Deload weeks:** Every 3-4 weeks, reduce TSS by 40-50%
- **Monotony:** Vary session types and intensities throughout the week
- **Rest days:** Minimum 1 complete rest day per week. 2 for athletes over 40.

### Nutrition
- Chronic carbohydrate restriction amplifies overtraining risk (Meeusen et al., 2013)
- Ensure adequate caloric intake — LEA (low energy availability) shares pathways with OTS (Stellingwerff et al., 2021)
- Post-ride nutrition within 60 minutes is protective

### Sleep
- Target 7-9 hours per night
- Sleep is the single most important recovery tool
- Poor sleep compounds training stress exponentially

### Life Stress
- Work stress, relationship stress, travel, and other life demands add to total allostatic load
- Training plans should account for known high-stress periods (reduce training, not increase)
- Athletes often need permission to train less during stressful life periods

## Response Protocol

### Mild Concerns (1-2 warning signs)
- Monitor closely for 3-5 days
- Reduce intensity but maintain volume
- Prioritize sleep and nutrition
- Reassess after one week

### Moderate Concerns (3+ warning signs)
- Reduce training load by 40-50% for 5-7 days
- No sessions above Zone 2
- Focus: sleep, nutrition, stress reduction
- Reassess at the end of the reduced week

### Severe Concerns (persistent symptoms >2 weeks, or red flags)
- Full rest for minimum 1 week
- Medical consultation recommended
- Nutritional assessment (screen for LEA/RED-S)
- Gradual return to training: Zone 1-2 only, rebuild slowly over 2-4 weeks
- Do NOT rush return to intensity
