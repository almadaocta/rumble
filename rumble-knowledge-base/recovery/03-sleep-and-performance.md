# Sleep and Athletic Performance

## Overview

Sleep is the highest-return recovery intervention available, and the one most often traded away. No supplement, modality, or protocol in this knowledge base competes with fixing a rider's sleep. It is also, unusually, free.

## What the Consensus Says

The 2021 expert consensus (Walsh et al.) reviewed the evidence and reached two conclusions worth foregrounding:

1. **Elite athletes are particularly susceptible to poor sleep** — habitual short sleep (under 7 hours per night) and poor sleep quality including fragmentation are common.
2. **A one-size-fits-all recommendation such as "7-9 hours" is unlikely to be ideal.** The consensus recommends an **individualised** approach that accounts for the athlete's own perceived sleep need.

This is an important nuance. The 7-9 hour figure is a reasonable starting point and a good default when nothing else is known, but the useful question is whether *this* rider is getting enough sleep for *their* needs — not whether they hit a population number.

## Why Sleep Matters for Cyclists

- **Physical recovery:** growth hormone release is concentrated in slow-wave sleep; muscle protein synthesis and tissue repair depend on it.
- **Glycogen resynthesis** and metabolic regulation are impaired by sleep restriction.
- **Immune function:** short sleep is one of the strongest predictors of upper respiratory infection in athletes.
- **Cognitive and motor performance:** reaction time, decision-making, and technical skill all degrade — which matters for bike handling and race tactics, not just power output.
- **Perceived exertion rises** after poor sleep even when physiological capacity is largely intact. The same session simply feels harder.
- **Injury risk increases** with chronic short sleep.
- **Mood, motivation, and training adherence** decline — often the first thing a coach notices.

## Assessing a Rider's Sleep

Ask about these before prescribing anything:

- **Duration:** typical time in bed and estimated actual sleep, on both training and rest nights.
- **Consistency:** how much do bedtime and wake time vary across the week? Large weekend shifts ("social jetlag") disrupt circadian rhythm as effectively as short sleep does.
- **Quality:** how long to fall asleep, how many awakenings, how refreshed on waking.
- **Timing:** is the rider a genuine late chronotype being forced into early sessions?
- **Daytime symptoms:** sleepiness, reliance on caffeine, difficulty concentrating.

Screening questionnaires are useful at the start of a season and mid-season. Wearable sleep-stage estimates are unreliable in detail but reasonable for tracking **duration and consistency trends** — use them for that, not for judging a night's "sleep score."

## Sleep Hygiene — What Actually Helps

**Consistency first.** A fixed wake time, seven days a week, does more than any other single change. The body's circadian system anchors on wake time far more than on bedtime.

**Environment**
- Dark, quiet, and cool — around 17-19°C suits most people.
- Blackout curtains or an eye mask; earplugs if the environment is noisy.
- Reserve the bed for sleep. Working, scrolling, and watching in bed weaken the association.

**Light**
- Bright light exposure in the morning, ideally outdoors, anchors the rhythm.
- Reduce bright and blue-enriched light in the 1-2 hours before bed.

**Timing of training, food, and stimulants**
- **Caffeine:** avoid within 6-8 hours of bedtime. This has direct implications for evening race-day caffeine use and for riders who caffeinate for late-afternoon sessions.
- **Hard evening sessions** elevate core temperature and sympathetic drive and can delay sleep onset. Where a rider must train late, allow 2-3 hours of wind-down before bed.
- **Alcohol** fragments sleep and suppresses REM even when it shortens the time to fall asleep. It is a sleep destroyer, not a sleep aid.
- **Avoid going to bed hungry** — a common problem for under-fuelling riders, and another link between low energy availability and poor recovery.

**Wind-down routine**
- A consistent 30-45 minute pre-sleep routine — low light, low stimulation, no work or racing-related admin.
- If awake for more than about 20 minutes, get up and do something quiet in low light rather than lying there. Lying awake in bed trains the wrong association.

## Napping

Naps are a legitimate recovery tool, particularly for riders with restricted overnight sleep or a heavy block:

- **20-30 minutes** avoids waking from deep sleep and the associated grogginess.
- **90 minutes** completes a full sleep cycle and is an alternative if time allows.
- Nap **early afternoon**. Naps after roughly 4pm degrade night sleep.
- A post-ride nap during a heavy training block or a stage race is genuinely valuable, not indulgent.

## Sleep Extension

Deliberately increasing sleep — going to bed earlier to add 1-2 hours — has produced performance improvements in athletes who were previously sleep-restricted. This is one of the few interventions where the "more is better" framing holds, up to the athlete's actual need.

**Recommend sleep extension when:** the rider is habitually under 7 hours, is entering a heavy block or training camp, is travelling across time zones, or is in the week before an A event (where extra sleep is more valuable than extra training).

## When to Escalate

Refer to a physician or sleep specialist for:
- Persistent insomnia lasting more than a few weeks despite good sleep hygiene
- Loud snoring, witnessed breathing pauses, or unrefreshing sleep despite adequate duration — possible sleep apnoea
- Excessive daytime sleepiness with apparently normal sleep duration
- Sleep problems accompanying depressed mood or anxiety

Do not recommend sleep medication. That is a physician's decision.

## Coaching Priorities

When a rider reports poor recovery, fatigue, or stalled progress, work in this order:

1. **Sleep duration and consistency** — the largest lever, and free.
2. **Energy and carbohydrate intake** — the second largest.
3. **Training load structure** — deloads, monotony, ramp rate.
4. **Everything else** — modalities, supplements, gadgets.

Riders and coaches routinely invert this list. A rider spending money on recovery boots while sleeping six hours has an ordering problem, not an equipment problem.
