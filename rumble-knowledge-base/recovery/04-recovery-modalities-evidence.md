# Recovery Modalities — What the Evidence Supports

## Overview

The recovery industry sells a great deal of equipment. The evidence supports much less of it than the marketing implies, and — critically — some modalities that reduce soreness also **reduce the training adaptation you were trying to produce**. That trade-off is the most important thing in this document.

The hierarchy that actually matters: **sleep, then nutrition, then load management, then everything below.** Modalities are a small refinement at the end of a well-constructed programme, not a substitute for one.

## The Two Questions to Ask About Any Modality

1. **Does it reduce soreness and perceived fatigue?** (Recovery of *feeling* and short-term readiness.)
2. **Does it interfere with adaptation?** (Recovery of *function* long term.)

A modality can score well on the first and badly on the second. Cold water immersion is the clearest example.

## What the Meta-Analysis Shows

Dupuy et al. (2018) meta-analysed 99 studies across ten recovery methods, measuring effects on delayed-onset muscle soreness, perceived fatigue, muscle damage markers, and inflammatory markers.

**Largest effects on soreness at 24, 48, and 72 hours: massage and cold water immersion.** Active recovery and compression garments produced smaller but real effects. Stretching performed poorly.

Note what these outcomes are: soreness, perceived fatigue, and biomarkers. They are **not** performance in the next session, and they are **not** long-term adaptation.

## Cold Water Immersion — Effective and Double-Edged

**What it does well:** reliably reduces soreness and perceived fatigue. Useful when the next performance matters more than the adaptation from the last one.

**The problem:** Roberts et al. (2015) had 21 physically active men strength train twice weekly for 12 weeks, using either 10 minutes of cold water immersion or active recovery after each session. The active recovery group gained more: isokinetic work increased 19%, type II fibre cross-sectional area 17%, and myonuclei per fibre 26% — **with no equivalent gains in the cold water immersion group.** Strength and muscle mass improved more with active recovery.

The mechanism is straightforward: the inflammatory and anabolic signalling that cold suppresses is part of how the adaptation happens.

### When to Use Cold Water Immersion

**Use it when the priority is the next performance:**
- Between stages of a multi-day race
- Between heats or sessions on the same day
- During a congested race block where recovery speed beats adaptation
- In extreme heat, for thermal recovery

**Avoid it when the priority is adaptation:**
- After gym sessions in a strength-building block — this is the clearest contraindication
- After key training sessions where you want maximal adaptive signal
- Habitually, as a default post-ride routine

**Protocol if used:** roughly 10-15 minutes at 11-15°C.

## Active Recovery

**Evidence:** modest but consistent reductions in soreness and perceived fatigue. Crucially, **no adaptation penalty** — and in the Roberts study, the active recovery group out-adapted the cold group.

**Protocol:** 20-40 minutes in Zone 1 (under 55% FTP), truly easy. The most common error is riding a "recovery ride" at tempo, which adds load instead of removing it.

**Verdict:** the default post-hard-session recovery tool. Cheap, safe, and it also maintains movement frequency and blood flow without cost.

## Massage

**Evidence:** among the strongest effects on soreness and perceived fatigue in the Dupuy meta-analysis. The mechanism is likely more neural and perceptual than mechanical — the "flushing lactic acid" explanation is not physiologically accurate.

**Verdict:** worth it if the athlete values it and can access it. Reduces soreness, feels good, no evidence of adaptation interference. Cost and availability are the limiting factors, not efficacy.

**Timing:** avoid deep tissue work in the 48 hours before a key event or race — it can leave legs feeling flat.

## Compression Garments

**Evidence:** small but real reductions in soreness and perceived fatigue. Effects on subsequent performance are inconsistent.

**Verdict:** low risk, low cost after purchase, modest benefit. Reasonable during long-haul travel and post-race. Not a priority purchase.

**Pneumatic compression boots:** popular, expensive, and the evidence is not meaningfully stronger than for static compression garments. If a rider enjoys them and can afford them, there is no harm — but they should not displace spending or attention from sleep and nutrition.

## Stretching

**Evidence:** performs poorly as a recovery modality. It does not meaningfully reduce soreness, and static stretching immediately before performance can transiently reduce force output.

**Verdict:** stretch for mobility and range of motion where a rider needs it — a legitimate goal with its own justification. Do not present it as recovery.

## Sauna and Heat Exposure

**Evidence:** weak as a direct recovery modality, but post-exercise heat exposure is a well-supported route to **heat acclimation** (see the cycling knowledge base). Plasma volume expansion is a real adaptation with performance value.

**Verdict:** use it as an adaptation tool, not as recovery. Do not use it when already dehydrated or in a heavy fatigue state.

## Nutrition as a Recovery Modality

Frequently overlooked in modality discussions and more effective than most of the list above:

- **Carbohydrate** within 30-60 minutes post-ride, especially with under 24 hours to the next session
- **Protein** — 0.3 g/kg post-session, distributed across the day
- **Fluid and sodium** replacement to 125-150% of the deficit

See the nutrition knowledge base for detail. A rider who nails post-ride nutrition and sleep and does nothing else will out-recover a rider with the full modality catalogue and neither.

## Practical Guidance by Scenario

| Scenario | Recommend | Avoid |
|---|---|---|
| Between stages of a stage race | Cold immersion, compression, massage, nutrition, sleep | Hard efforts, alcohol |
| After a key gym session in a build block | Nutrition, sleep, light active recovery | Cold water immersion |
| After a hard interval session, next session in 48h | Active recovery, nutrition, sleep | Nothing needed beyond basics |
| Chronic fatigue accumulating over weeks | Load reduction, sleep audit, energy availability screen | Adding modalities to mask the problem |
| Long-haul travel to an event | Compression, hydration, sleep timing strategy | Sedentary immobility |

## The Framing to Give Athletes

Modalities are worth using when they are cheap, enjoyable, and non-interfering. They become a problem in two ways: when they displace attention from sleep, fuelling, and sensible load management, and when they are used habitually in a way that blunts adaptation.

**If a rider is chronically sore, exhausted, or stalled, the answer is almost never a new recovery device. It is less training, more food, or more sleep** — and often all three.
