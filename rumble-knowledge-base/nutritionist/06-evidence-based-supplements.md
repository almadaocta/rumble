# Evidence-Based Supplements for Cyclists

## Overview

The supplement market is enormous and the evidence base is small. Only a handful of substances have strong, repeatedly replicated evidence for performance benefit in endurance sport. Everything else ranges from "plausible but unproven" to "money for expensive urine." This document covers the short list that survives scrutiny, with doses and timing.

**Nothing here substitutes for the fundamentals.** Adequate energy, carbohydrate periodisation, protein, sleep, and consistent training account for the overwhelming majority of performance. Supplements are a small final percentage on top of that, and they only pay out when the foundation is already in place.

## Tier 1 — Strong Evidence

### Caffeine

The most reliably ergogenic legal supplement for endurance performance (Guest et al., 2021).

- **Dose:** 3-6 mg/kg body mass. A 75 kg rider: roughly 225-450 mg.
- **Timing:** approximately 60 minutes before the effort for capsules or tablets; faster for chewing gum forms.
- **Effect:** improved endurance performance, reduced perceived exertion, improved vigilance and reaction time. Benefits hold for endurance exercise in the heat (3-6 mg/kg) and at altitude (4-6 mg/kg).
- **Higher doses are not better.** Above about 9 mg/kg the side effects — jitteriness, elevated heart rate, GI upset, impaired sleep — outweigh any further benefit.
- **Individual variation is large**, partly genetic. Some riders respond strongly, some barely at all, some tolerate it poorly.
- **During long rides:** smaller doses (1-2 mg/kg) taken late in a long event are effective and often better tolerated than one large pre-ride dose.

**Practical cautions:** caffeine taken after early afternoon degrades that night's sleep, which for a multi-day event is a net loss. Always trial in training before racing.

### Creatine Monohydrate

Best established for high-intensity, repeated-effort work rather than steady-state endurance (Kreider et al., 2017).

- **Dose:** either a loading protocol of ~20 g/day split into 4 doses for 5-7 days followed by 3-5 g/day maintenance, or simply 3-5 g/day from the start (reaching saturation in about 3-4 weeks).
- **Relevance to cyclists:** useful for track, criterium, and sprint-oriented riders, and supportive of gym-based strength work in the off-season. Little direct benefit for long steady efforts.
- **The weight consideration:** creatine typically causes 1-2 kg of intracellular water retention. For a climber targeting watts per kilogram, that trade-off may not be worth it. For a sprinter or a rider in an off-season strength block, it usually is.
- **Safety:** among the most extensively studied supplements available, with a strong safety record in healthy individuals, including long-term use.

## Tier 2 — Good Evidence, Narrower Application

### Dietary Nitrate (Beetroot Juice)

- **Dose:** approximately **6-8 mmol nitrate**, typically one concentrated 70 mL beetroot "shot."
- **Timing:** **2-3 hours before** exercise for an acute dose. Multi-day loading (3-7 days) may produce a larger effect than a single dose.
- **Effect:** reduced oxygen cost of submaximal exercise and improved time to exhaustion at submaximal workloads (Jones, 2014).
- **Who benefits most:** recreational and moderately trained riders. **The effect diminishes in highly trained endurance athletes** — several studies in elite cyclists show no benefit. Manage expectations accordingly.
- **Caution:** antibacterial mouthwash blocks the oral bacteria that convert nitrate to nitrite and abolishes the effect. Avoid mouthwash on supplementation days.

### Beta-Alanine

- **Dose:** **4-6 g/day for at least 2-4 weeks**, split into smaller doses (Trexler et al., 2015).
- **Mechanism:** raises muscle carnosine, improving intracellular buffering.
- **Effect:** most pronounced for efforts of **1-4 minutes**. Relevant for pursuit riders, criterium racers, and repeated hard climbing efforts; not for long steady-state work.
- **Side effect:** paraesthesia (harmless skin tingling). Mitigated by splitting into doses of ~1.6 g or using sustained-release formulations.
- **This is a loading supplement, not a race-day one.** There is no acute pre-race dose.

### Sodium Bicarbonate

- **Dose:** **0.2-0.3 g/kg body mass**, taken 60-180 minutes before the effort (Grgic et al., 2021).
- **Effect:** improves performance in high-intensity efforts of roughly 1-10 minutes by raising extracellular buffering capacity.
- **Major caveat:** **GI distress is common and can be severe** — cramping, nausea, diarrhoea. Must be trialled repeatedly in training. Splitting the dose, taking it with a carbohydrate-rich meal, or using enteric-coated forms reduces the problem.
- **May stack additively with creatine or beta-alanine.**
- For most amateur road cyclists this is high-risk, moderate-reward. Reserve it for riders in short, intense events who have tolerated it in training.

## Supplements With Legitimate Non-Performance Roles

These treat deficiencies rather than enhance performance in a replete athlete — which makes them important, but for a different reason.

- **Iron:** critical for endurance athletes, particularly female athletes and those training at altitude. **Only supplement on the basis of blood work** — ferritin, transferrin saturation, and haemoglobin. Unnecessary iron supplementation is harmful.
- **Vitamin D:** widespread insufficiency in northern latitudes and in indoor-training riders. Test and correct rather than supplement blindly.
- **Vitamin B12:** relevant for vegan and vegetarian riders. Supplementation is non-optional on a fully plant-based diet.
- **Calcium:** relevant to cyclists specifically because of the sport's poor bone-loading profile (see the strength and conditioning knowledge base on bone health).

## Antioxidants — A Caution

High-dose antioxidant supplementation, particularly **vitamin C and vitamin E**, can blunt the training adaptations that exercise-induced oxidative stress helps signal. Riders should get antioxidants from food rather than high-dose supplements during training blocks.

## What Does Not Have Convincing Evidence

Commonly marketed to cyclists without robust support for endurance performance: BCAAs (in an athlete meeting total protein needs), glutamine, ketone esters (equivocal and expensive), most "fat burner" formulations, testosterone boosters, and the majority of proprietary blends. Absence of evidence is not proof of no effect, but it is a poor basis for spending money or accepting contamination risk.

## Contamination and Anti-Doping

For any rider subject to testing, contamination is a real and documented risk. Supplements are not regulated to pharmaceutical standards and cross-contamination with banned substances occurs.

- Use only products carrying **third-party batch testing** (Informed Sport, NSF Certified for Sport, or equivalent).
- Strict liability applies — "it was in my supplement" is not a defence.
- When in doubt, don't.

## How to Advise on Supplements

1. **Audit the fundamentals first.** A rider sleeping six hours and under-fuelling does not have a supplement problem.
2. **One change at a time.** Adding three supplements simultaneously makes it impossible to know what worked.
3. **Trial in training, never on race day.** This applies without exception, most emphatically to bicarbonate and caffeine.
4. **Match the supplement to the event.** Beta-alanine and bicarbonate do nothing for a five-hour gran fondo; nitrate and caffeine do nothing for a 10-second sprint.
5. **Recommend blood work before iron or vitamin D**, always.
