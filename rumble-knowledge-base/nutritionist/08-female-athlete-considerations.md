# Female Athlete Considerations

## Overview

Most sports science was done on men. Elliott-Sale et al. (2021) documented how thin and methodologically weak the female-specific evidence base is — a large share of studies fail to verify menstrual cycle phase with hormone measurement, which makes their conclusions unreliable. This document is therefore written with an explicit distinction between **what is well established** and **what is plausible but unproven**, because overstating the second category is how female athletes get given confident advice that turns out to be wrong.

## What Is Well Established

- **Energy availability matters at least as much, and the consequences of getting it wrong are better documented in women.** Menstrual dysfunction is one of the earliest and clearest signals of problematic low energy availability (see the energy availability and REDs document).
- **Iron deficiency is substantially more common in female endurance athletes**, driven by menstrual losses on top of the general endurance-athlete risk factors.
- **Bone health interacts with both.** Menstrual dysfunction plus cycling's inherently poor bone-loading profile is a genuinely high-risk combination for low bone mineral density.
- **Absolute protein requirements scale with body and fat-free mass**, not with sex per se. The g/kg targets in the protein document apply to female riders unchanged.

## What Is Less Certain — The Menstrual Cycle and Performance

McNulty et al. (2020) meta-analysed the effect of menstrual cycle phase on exercise performance in eumenorrheic (naturally menstruating) women. The headline findings:

- Performance **may be trivially reduced during the early follicular phase** — the days at and just after the onset of menstruation — compared with other phases.
- **The average effect was trivial in magnitude**, with substantial overlap between phases.
- **Study quality was poor.** Of 78 studies included, only 8% were rated high quality and 68% were rated low or very low quality.

### How to Use This

**Do not build a training plan around menstrual cycle phase as though the physiology were settled.** The group-level effect is small and the individual variation swamps it.

**Do take individual symptoms seriously.** A rider who reliably feels terrible and performs badly on days 1-2 of her cycle is describing a real, individual pattern — and that pattern is actionable even though the group-level literature is weak. The correct response is individual tracking, not a generic phase-based template.

### Practical Approach

1. **Track the cycle alongside training data** — cycle day, symptoms, sleep, RPE relative to prescribed intensity, and session outcome.
2. **Look for a repeatable individual pattern across at least 3-4 cycles.** One bad session on day 2 is noise.
3. **If a pattern exists, plan around it:** schedule key sessions and testing away from consistently symptomatic days, and place recovery or endurance work there instead.
4. **If no pattern exists, don't invent one.** Many riders have no consistent phase effect at all.
5. **Never let cycle tracking become another source of anxiety or a reason to skip training pre-emptively.**

### Hormonal Contraception

Riders on hormonal contraception do not have a natural cycle, and the phase literature does not apply to them. Absence of menstruation while on hormonal contraception is not a REDs signal in the way that absent menstruation off contraception is — which also means **hormonal contraception can mask one of the clearest warning signs of low energy availability**. For these riders, other REDs indicators (bone stress injury, recurrent illness, performance decline, fatigue) carry more diagnostic weight.

## Iron — The Highest-Value Intervention

Iron deficiency is common, meaningfully impairs endurance performance, and is straightforward to detect and correct.

### Screening

- **Test ferritin, haemoglobin, and transferrin saturation** at least annually; twice yearly for riders with a history of deficiency, heavy menstrual bleeding, or vegetarian/vegan diets.
- Ferritin is an acute-phase reactant — it rises with inflammation and infection, so a value taken during illness or immediately after a hard block can be misleadingly high.
- **Interpretation belongs to a physician.** Do not set treatment thresholds from a knowledge base.

### Dietary Support

- **Haem iron** (red meat, poultry, fish) is absorbed far more efficiently than **non-haem iron** (legumes, leafy greens, fortified grains).
- **Vitamin C substantially improves non-haem absorption** — pair plant iron sources with citrus, peppers, or tomatoes.
- **Tea, coffee, and calcium inhibit absorption.** Separate them from iron-rich meals by an hour or two.
- Vegetarian and vegan riders should assume a higher requirement and monitor more closely.

### Supplementation

**Only on the basis of blood work and medical advice.** Iron supplementation without deficiency provides no benefit and iron overload is harmful. Where indicated, alternate-day dosing is often better absorbed and better tolerated than daily dosing, and GI side effects are common enough to warrant planning for them.

## Energy Availability — The Recurring Theme

Everything above connects back to energy availability. The most common pattern in female endurance cyclists is a slow drift into chronic under-fuelling, which then presents as menstrual disturbance, low ferritin, poor bone density, frequent illness, and a performance plateau — often attributed to "needing to train harder."

**When a female rider presents with any two of: menstrual disturbance, recurrent bone stress injury, low ferritin, frequent illness, or unexplained performance decline — screen for low energy availability and refer.**

## Communication

- Keep the framing on health and performance. Never on appearance or weight.
- Menstrual health is performance data, and normalising the conversation about it is part of good coaching — but the athlete sets the terms of how much they want to discuss.
- Be explicit about uncertainty. "The research here is genuinely thin, so let's track your own data" is a more useful and more honest answer than a confident phase-based prescription.
