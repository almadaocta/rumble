# During-Ride Fueling Guide

## Overview

Carbohydrate intake during cycling is one of the most impactful nutrition interventions. The right amount and type of carbs during a ride can improve performance by 2-8% compared to water alone. Guidelines depend on ride duration and intensity.

## Fueling by Duration — The Jeukendrup Framework

Based on Jeukendrup (2014), Sports Medicine, 44(S1):S25-33.

### Rides Under 45 Minutes
- **Carbs needed:** None
- **Action:** Water only (if needed at all)
- **Rationale:** Muscle glycogen stores are sufficient. Even high-intensity short efforts don't deplete glycogen enough to need exogenous carbs.

### Rides 45-75 Minutes (High Intensity)
- **Carbs needed:** Mouth rinse or small amounts
- **Action:** Swish a carb drink and spit, or sip small amounts (up to 20g)
- **Rationale:** Performance benefit comes from CNS activation via oral carb receptors, not metabolic fuel delivery. Rinsing a carb solution activates brain regions that improve motor output.

### Rides 1-2.5 Hours
- **Carbs needed:** 30-60 g/hour
- **Carb type:** Any single source (glucose, maltodextrin, sucrose)
- **Action:** Start fueling within first 30-45 minutes. Don't wait until hungry.
- **Practical:** 1 gel (25g) + sips of carb drink (20-30g) per hour. Or 1 energy bar per hour.

### Rides 2.5-3+ Hours
- **Carbs needed:** 60-90 g/hour
- **Carb type:** Multiple transportable carbohydrates (glucose:fructose ratio of 2:1 or maltodextrin:fructose)
- **Why multiple sources:** Glucose absorption is limited to ~60g/h by the SGLT1 transporter. Adding fructose (which uses the GLUT5 transporter) allows total absorption up to ~90g/h — a 50-75% increase.
- **Practical:** Specialized high-carb drink mixes (80-100g/bottle) + gels as needed.

### Rides 4+ Hours / Ultra
- **Carbs needed:** 80-90+ g/hour (if tolerated)
- **Carb type:** Multiple transportable carbohydrates
- **Additional:** Include some solid food (rice cakes, sandwiches) for satiety and gut comfort. Mix liquid and solid carb sources.

## Quick Reference Table

| Duration | Carbs/Hour | Type | Delivery |
|----------|-----------|------|----------|
| <45 min | None | - | Water if needed |
| 45-75 min | Mouth rinse or 0-20g | Any | Swish or sip |
| 1-2.5 hours | 30-60g | Single source | Drink + gel |
| 2.5-3+ hours | 60-90g | Glucose:fructose 2:1 | Drink mix + gels |
| 4+ hours | 80-90g+ | Multiple transportable | Mix + solid food |

## Gut Training

High carb intake (60-90g/h) requires gut training — the intestine adapts to absorb more carbs with practice.

### Protocol
- Start at 30-40g/h during training rides
- Increase by 10g/h each week
- Over 4-6 weeks, build to 60-90g/h
- Practice with the exact products you'll use in races
- Train fueling on hard sessions, not just easy rides

### GI Distress Prevention
- Avoid high-fat and high-fiber foods in the 3 hours before a long ride
- Practice race nutrition in training — never try new products on race day
- Start fueling early (within 20-30 minutes of starting the ride)
- Drink consistently rather than large boluses
- If GI issues occur, reduce intake temporarily and rebuild

## Beyond 90 g/h — The High-Carbohydrate Frontier

The 90 g/h figure was long treated as a hard ceiling set by intestinal transporter capacity. More recent work has pushed past it, and professional endurance sport has moved with it.

### What the Evidence Shows

- Podlogar and Wallis (2022) reviewed the emerging case for intakes above the traditional ceiling, reporting **higher exogenous carbohydrate oxidation with glucose-fructose blends ingested at 120 g/h compared with 90 g/h**. The ratio matters at these rates — as absolute intake rises, a higher proportion of fructose (approaching 1:0.8 glucose:fructose) is used rather than the classic 2:1.
- Viribay et al. (2020) studied elite trail runners during a mountain marathon at 120, 90, and 60 g/h, and found **reduced markers of exercise-induced muscle damage and better preserved post-exercise neuromuscular function** in the 120 g/h group.

### Who This Applies To

Very high intakes are appropriate for a narrow set of situations:

- **Long, hard events** — 4+ hours at genuine racing intensity, stage races, ultra-endurance events.
- **Riders who have completed a full gut-training progression** and can demonstrate tolerance in training.
- **Riders using purpose-built high-carbohydrate products.** Reaching 120 g/h from standard gels means roughly five gels per hour, which is not realistic.

### Who This Does Not Apply To

- **Riders doing 2-3 hour training rides.** There is no benefit and a real cost in GI distress and unnecessary calories.
- **Riders who have not gut-trained.** Attempting 120 g/h untrained reliably produces GI failure and a worse outcome than 60 g/h would have.
- **Riders in a body-composition-focused block** — though note that under-fuelling key sessions is counterproductive (see the energy availability document).

### Practical Guidance

Treat 120 g/h as the **top of an extended range, not a new default**. The progression is unchanged: build gradually over 4-6 weeks of gut training, extend the same protocol beyond 90 g/h only if the athlete is tolerating 90 comfortably, and stop escalating at the point where the gut objects. **The best fuelling rate is the highest one the athlete can absorb without GI distress on the day** — for many riders that remains 60-90 g/h, and that is a perfectly good answer.

## Hydration During Rides

- **General target:** 400-800 ml/hour depending on conditions (heat, humidity, sweat rate)
- **Carb drinks count** toward both hydration and fueling targets
- **Electrolytes:** Include sodium (300-600 mg/L) in drinks for rides >2 hours or in hot conditions
- **Individual sweat rates vary** significantly (0.5-2.5 L/hour). Weigh before and after rides to estimate personal sweat rate.
