# Energy Availability and Relative Energy Deficiency in Sport (REDs)

## Overview

Low energy availability is the most consequential nutrition problem in endurance sport, and it is routinely mistaken for overtraining, poor discipline, or bad luck with illness. It affects male and female athletes. Cycling — a weight-sensitive, high-expenditure, low-impact sport — is one of the higher-risk environments for it.

**This is the topic where a nutrition specialist should be most willing to escalate to a physician.** Suspected REDs is a medical matter, not a coaching adjustment.

## The Core Concept

**Energy availability (EA) = (energy intake − exercise energy expenditure) ÷ fat-free mass**, expressed in kcal per kg FFM per day.

It describes the energy left over for basic physiological function *after* training has taken its share. A rider can be eating a large absolute number of calories and still be in low energy availability if training expenditure is high enough.

### Reference Thresholds

- **Roughly 45 kcal/kg FFM/day:** the approximate level associated with energy balance and normal physiological function in most athletes.
- **Below approximately 30 kcal/kg FFM/day:** the commonly cited threshold below which measurable disruptions to hormonal, metabolic, and bone function appear.

**Treat these numbers as orientation, not diagnosis.** The 2023 IOC consensus (Mountjoy et al.) explicitly moved away from a single hard cut-off, framing low energy availability as a **spectrum from adaptable to problematic**, with individual variation in where the harm begins. A rider is not "fine" at 31 and "in REDs" at 29.

### Worked Example

A 70 kg rider with 12% body fat has roughly 62 kg fat-free mass.
- Eating 2,800 kcal/day, riding 3 hours at ~700 kcal/h = 2,100 kcal exercise expenditure.
- EA = (2,800 − 2,100) ÷ 62 = **~11 kcal/kg FFM/day**.

That is severe low energy availability — despite the rider consuming what sounds like a generous 2,800 kcal.

## REDs — What Low Energy Availability Causes

REDs is the syndrome of impaired health and performance resulting from sustained problematic low energy availability. It is not exclusive to women, not exclusive to elite athletes, and not exclusive to riders with eating disorders — though disordered eating is one common pathway in.

### Health Consequences

- **Bone:** reduced bone mineral density, stress fractures, impaired bone formation. Compounding an already serious issue in cyclists.
- **Endocrine:** menstrual dysfunction and amenorrhoea in female athletes; suppressed testosterone in male athletes.
- **Metabolic:** reduced resting metabolic rate, impaired glucose and lipid metabolism.
- **Immune:** more frequent illness, slower resolution.
- **Gastrointestinal, cardiovascular, haematological, and psychological** effects are all documented.
- **Growth and development** effects in adolescent athletes.

### Performance Consequences

- Decreased endurance performance and training response
- Reduced muscle strength and glycogen storage
- Impaired coordination, concentration, and judgement
- Increased injury risk and prolonged recovery
- Depression, irritability, and reduced training motivation

Crucially, these overlap almost completely with overtraining syndrome. Stellingwerff et al. (2021) documented the shared pathways. **A rider presenting with "overtraining" should always be screened for low energy availability.**

## Warning Signs to Screen For

**Physiological**
- Menstrual cycle changes, irregularity, or absence (in athletes not on hormonal contraception)
- Recurrent stress fractures or bone stress injuries
- Unexplained fatigue not resolving with rest
- Frequent illness, particularly upper respiratory infections
- Always feeling cold; low resting heart rate paired with poor performance
- Loss of libido; in male riders, symptoms of low testosterone
- Poor or declining bone density on scan

**Behavioural**
- Rigid food rules, food anxiety, or avoidance of entire macronutrient groups
- Training through illness and injury; compulsive exercise
- Rapid or continued weight loss, particularly in-season
- Skipping post-ride nutrition; training fasted routinely rather than deliberately
- Preoccupation with weight, body composition, or power-to-weight numbers
- Social withdrawal around meals

**Performance**
- Plateau or decline despite consistent, well-structured training
- Loss of high-end power specifically
- Poor recovery between sessions

## Screening and Escalation

- **Screening tools exist** — the LEAF-Q for female athletes and RED-S risk assessment frameworks — but they are screens, not diagnoses.
- **Refer to a physician** for: amenorrhoea or menstrual disturbance, recurrent bone stress injury, suspected eating disorder, or any cluster of the health consequences above.
- **Refer to a registered sports dietitian** for structured intake assessment and a refeeding plan.
- **Suspected eating disorder is always a medical and psychological referral.** Do not attempt to manage it through meal planning alone.

## Prevention and Correction

### Prevention

- **Fuel for the work required** — match intake to the day's demands rather than to a fixed daily number (see the carbohydrate targets document).
- Never combine a large energy deficit with a high-intensity training block. If weight change is a goal, pursue it in base season, slowly, at 0.25-0.5% of body mass per week.
- Protect post-ride nutrition. Skipping it is one of the most common routes into chronic deficit.
- Weigh the athlete's total load, not just their calories — high life stress raises the cost of a given training week.

### Correction

- **Increase energy intake before reducing training**, where the situation allows it. The goal is to raise availability, and the athlete usually values training.
- Where the deficit is severe or health markers are affected, **training load must come down as well**, under medical guidance.
- Recovery timelines are long: menstrual function and bone density can take **months to years** to normalise. This is not a two-week fix, and framing it as one sets the athlete up to abandon the plan.
- Reintroduce carbohydrate deliberately — most riders in low energy availability are under-eating carbohydrate specifically.

## Talking About It

The way this conversation is handled matters as much as the content.

- Frame it around **performance and health**, not appearance or weight.
- Avoid moralising language about food. Avoid praising weight loss.
- Do not set body-composition targets for an athlete showing any warning signs.
- Take the athlete's reported symptoms seriously even when their power numbers still look acceptable — performance is often the *last* thing to fall.
