# Hydration and Electrolytes

## Overview

Hydration guidance has swung between two errors: "drink as much as possible" (which caused fatal hyponatraemia cases in endurance events) and "drink only to thirst" (which under-serves riders with high sweat rates in the heat). The defensible position sits between them — replace enough to stay within about 2% of starting body mass, individualised to measured sweat rate.

## The Core Guideline

Both the ACSM (Sawka et al., 2007) and the National Athletic Trainers' Association (McDermott et al., 2017) converge on the same target:

**Limit body-mass loss during exercise to less than 2%, and avoid gaining weight during exercise.**

- Losses beyond ~2% progressively impair endurance performance, and the impairment is larger in the heat.
- **Gaining** weight during a ride means fluid intake has exceeded sweat losses — the primary risk factor for exercise-associated hyponatraemia, which is dangerous.
- The 2% figure is a practical guardrail, not a cliff edge. A rider 2.5% down is not in crisis; a rider 6% down is.

## Measuring Sweat Rate

This is the single most useful hydration measurement, and it takes one ride:

1. Weigh the rider nude before the ride (after voiding).
2. Ride for a known duration, recording all fluid consumed in litres.
3. Weigh nude again immediately after, towelled dry.

**Sweat rate (L/h) = (pre-ride kg − post-ride kg + fluid consumed in L) ÷ hours**

Repeat in different conditions — cool, hot, high intensity — since sweat rate varies enormously with all three.

### Typical Values

- **Cool conditions, moderate intensity:** 0.4-0.8 L/h
- **Warm conditions or hard riding:** 0.8-1.5 L/h
- **Hot conditions, hard riding:** 1.5-2.5 L/h in heavy sweaters

Individual variation is very large. A 0.5 L/h rider and a 2.0 L/h rider need completely different plans, and generic "one bottle per hour" advice fails both.

## Practical Intake Targets

- **Aim to replace roughly 70-100% of sweat losses** during the ride. Full replacement is often not achievable at high sweat rates, and modest deficits are tolerable.
- **Do not exceed sweat rate.** Drinking more than you sweat provides no benefit and creates risk.
- **Rides under about 60-75 minutes** in cool conditions: drinking to thirst is sufficient.
- **Gastric tolerance ceiling:** most riders tolerate roughly **0.8-1.2 L/h** of fluid. Riders sweating 2 L/h cannot fully replace losses and must accept a deficit — plan for it rather than force-drinking.

## Sodium

Sweat sodium concentration varies roughly **from 200 to 2,000 mg per litre** between individuals, and is one of the most individually variable parameters in sports nutrition. Heat acclimation reduces it.

### When Sodium Matters

Sodium replacement becomes meaningful when:
- Exercise exceeds about **2 hours**, or
- Conditions are hot and sweat rate is high, or
- The rider is a **"salty sweater"** — visible white residue on kit and skin, gritty taste, history of cramping.

### Targets

- **General guidance:** 300-600 mg sodium per litre of fluid consumed for rides over 2 hours.
- **Heavy/salty sweaters in hot conditions:** 800-1,500 mg per litre.
- Most commercial sports drinks supply roughly 300-500 mg/L — often adequate for moderate conditions, frequently insufficient for hot long days.

### On Cramping

The evidence linking sodium loss and dehydration to exercise-associated muscle cramps is weaker than the marketing suggests; neuromuscular fatigue is at least as plausible a cause. Sodium is worth trying for a rider with a cramping history, but if cramps persist, investigate pacing, training specificity, and total workload rather than escalating electrolyte products indefinitely.

## Hyponatraemia — The Risk on the Other Side

Exercise-associated hyponatraemia results from drinking beyond sweat losses, diluting blood sodium. It is more common in slower participants in long events who have prolonged drinking opportunities.

**Warning signs:** nausea, headache, confusion, bloating, puffy hands and face, and — critically — **weight gain during the event**.

**Prevention:** never drink beyond thirst *and* beyond measured sweat rate; include sodium in fluids on long hot rides; never weigh more after a ride than before.

Suspected hyponatraemia is a medical emergency. Do not treat it with more plain water.

## Daily Hydration Status

- **Urine colour** is a reasonable rough field check: pale straw is the target, dark amber suggests under-hydration. First morning urine is normally darker and is not a good sample.
- **Morning body mass** trending down over consecutive days without a dietary reason suggests accumulating fluid deficit.
- **Baseline daily fluid** for a training cyclist: roughly 30-40 mL per kg body mass, *plus* replacement of training losses. A 75 kg rider on a rest day therefore needs roughly 2.2-3.0 L total from all sources.
- Caffeine at habitual doses does not cause meaningful net dehydration in trained individuals.

## Pre- and Post-Ride Protocols

**Pre-ride (2-4 hours before):** 5-7 mL/kg of fluid — roughly 375-525 mL for a 75 kg rider. Top up with a further 3-5 mL/kg two hours out if urine is still dark. Include sodium with the pre-ride fluid before hot events to improve retention.

**Post-ride:** replace **125-150% of the fluid deficit** over the following 2-4 hours. A rider 1 kg down should consume roughly 1.25-1.5 L. The extra volume covers ongoing urine losses. Include sodium — either through the drink or through food eaten alongside — or much of the fluid will simply be excreted.

## Hot-Weather Additions

- Pre-cooling with cold fluids or ice slurry before the start reduces thermal strain.
- Cold drinks are absorbed as well as room-temperature drinks and are usually consumed in greater volume.
- Heat acclimation increases sweat rate but *reduces* sweat sodium concentration — a well-acclimated rider needs more fluid but proportionally less sodium than they did unacclimated.
