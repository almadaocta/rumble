# Tapering and Peaking for Target Events

## Overview

A taper is a progressive, non-linear reduction of training load before a target event, designed to shed accumulated fatigue while preserving the fitness that was built. Done well it is worth roughly 3% of performance — the difference between a podium and mid-pack. Done badly it either leaves the rider flat or leaves them fatigued.

## What the Evidence Says

Mujika and Padilla (2003) reviewed the physiological basis of tapering and Bosquet et al. (2007) meta-analysed 27 controlled studies. The consistent findings:

- **Expected gain:** about **3% on average**, typical range 0.5-6.0%.
- **Reduce volume, not intensity.** Training volume can fall by 40-60% (and in some protocols up to 90% for very short tapers). Intensity must be maintained — dropping intensity is the single most common way to lose the taper's benefit.
- **Keep frequency high.** Session frequency should drop by no more than about 20%. Riders need to keep touching the bike.
- **Optimal duration is around 2 weeks** for most endurance athletes, with the useful range spanning roughly 4 to 28 days depending on the preceding training load.
- **Progressive (exponential) reductions beat step reductions.** A taper that decays load gradually outperforms one that cuts load abruptly and holds it flat.

The mechanisms behind the gain span cardiorespiratory, metabolic, haematological, hormonal, neuromuscular, and psychological systems — it is not simply "being rested."

## Choosing Taper Length

| Preceding block | Event importance | Taper length |
|---|---|---|
| Normal training, no heavy overload | B or C priority race | 5-7 days |
| Standard build block | A-priority one-day event | 10-14 days |
| Heavy overload / training camp | A-priority event | 14-21 days |
| Stage race or multi-day target | A-priority | 10-14 days, arriving slightly less fresh |

The deeper the accumulated fatigue, the longer the taper needs to be. A rider who has not been training hard has nothing to taper from — cutting volume just detrains them.

## Taper Structure — 2 Week Template

**Week -2 (first taper week): reduce volume ~30-40%**
- Keep two intensity sessions, but cut the total interval volume roughly in half. Example: a 5 × 5 min VO2max session becomes 3 × 5 min at the same power.
- Keep one moderate endurance ride, shortened.
- Add one extra rest or easy day compared to the build block.

**Week -1 (race week): reduce volume ~50-60% from normal**
- Two short openers containing brief efforts at or above race intensity — e.g. 3 × 3 min at threshold, or 5 × 1 min at VO2max power.
- All other riding in Zone 1-2, short.
- Complete rest 2 days before the event for most riders.

**Day before the event: openers**
- 45-75 min easy with 3-5 × 1-2 min at race intensity plus a few 10-second accelerations.
- Purpose is neuromuscular priming, not fitness. It should feel easy and short.

## Managing TSB Through the Taper

Using the Performance Management Chart framework (see the recovery knowledge base on TSB interpretation):

- **Target TSB on race day: +5 to +25** for most one-day events.
- CTL will drop slightly during a taper. **This is expected and acceptable** — a 2-5 point CTL loss in exchange for a 25-30 point TSB swing is a good trade.
- **Do not chase CTL during a taper.** Riders who panic about a falling CTL and add volume in race week routinely underperform.
- Riders who habitually race well on low freshness (criterium and stage-race types) may prefer TSB around 0 to +10. Riders who need to feel sparkling (time-triallists, climbers targeting one effort) sit higher.

## Individualisation

There is no universal taper. Track it:

- After each A event, record the taper length, the volume reduction, race-day TSB, and how the rider actually felt and performed.
- **Flat legs, no snap, low heart rate, low motivation** → tapered too long or cut intensity. Shorten the next taper and keep more intensity.
- **Heavy legs, high heart rate, poor repeatability** → tapered too short. Lengthen the next taper.
- Two or three well-documented tapers give a rider a reusable personal protocol. This is one of the highest-return records an athlete can keep.

## Common Taper Mistakes

- **Cutting intensity along with volume.** The most common and most costly error.
- **Adding a "confidence session."** A hard session in the last 4-5 days does not add fitness and reliably subtracts freshness.
- **Cramming missed training.** Training missed in the build block is gone. Race week cannot recover it.
- **Changing anything new.** New equipment, new nutrition products, new position, new routine — the taper is the worst possible time.
- **Sleeping less.** Reduced training frees up time; that time should go to sleep, not to more life stress.
- **Over-eating without adjusting.** Volume falls but appetite lags. For most events, carbohydrate intake should stay high in the final 48h (carbohydrate loading) while total intake earlier in the taper drops modestly.

## Peaking Across a Season

A rider can hold a genuine peak for roughly **2-3 weeks**, and can produce perhaps **2-3 real peaks per season**. Attempting to peak for every event produces a flat season with no highs.

- Designate A events first, then build the season backwards from them.
- Between peaks, return to a short build block of 3-5 weeks before tapering again.
- After the final A event of a block, take a genuine transition period: 1-2 weeks of unstructured, minimal riding.
