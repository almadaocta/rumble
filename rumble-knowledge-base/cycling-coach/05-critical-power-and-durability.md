# Critical Power, W′, and Durability

## Overview

FTP is a practical anchor, but the underlying physiology is better described by the power-duration relationship. Critical Power (CP) and W′ ("W prime") describe *how long* a rider can hold *any* given power, not just one hour. Durability describes how much of that capacity survives after three hours of riding. Together they explain why two riders with identical FTPs can perform completely differently in a real race.

## The Power-Duration Relationship

Maximal sustainable power is not a single number — it is a hyperbolic curve with two parameters (Jones et al., 2010; Poole et al., 2016):

- **Critical Power (CP):** the asymptote of the curve. The highest metabolic steady state — the boundary above which VO2 and blood lactate can no longer stabilise and exhaustion becomes inevitable. Expressed in watts.
- **W′:** the fixed amount of work (in kilojoules) that can be performed *above* CP before exhaustion. Think of it as a battery: it depletes when riding above CP and recharges when riding below.

The relationship: **time to exhaustion = W′ / (P − CP)** for any power P above CP.

### CP vs FTP — Not the Same Thing

CP and FTP are close but not identical. CP is typically **a few percent higher than FTP** because CP is derived from maximal efforts while FTP is defined around a one-hour quasi-steady-state effort. Riders can usually hold CP for roughly 20-40 minutes, not 60. Treat FTP as the training-prescription anchor and CP/W′ as the diagnostic lens.

### Typical Values

- **W′ for trained cyclists:** roughly 15-25 kJ. Sprinters and pursuit-type riders sit at the high end; pure climbers and time-triallists at the low end.
- **A rider with high CP and low W′** excels at long sustained climbs and time trials but gets shelled by repeated attacks.
- **A rider with lower CP and large W′** survives surges and sprints well but fades on long climbs.

## Testing CP and W′

CP and W′ require at least two — preferably three — maximal efforts of different durations, all on the same terrain and within a few days:

- **Short effort:** 3 minutes all-out
- **Medium effort:** 8-12 minutes all-out
- **Long effort:** 20 minutes all-out (optional third point, improves the model fit)

Fit the two-parameter model to those points. Efforts shorter than about 2 minutes or longer than about 20 minutes distort the fit — the model breaks down at both extremes.

**Practical alternative:** modern head units and platforms estimate CP/W′ continuously from ride data. These estimates are only as good as the maximal efforts in the file — a rider who never goes deep will get a lowballed CP.

## Coaching Applications of W′

### Pacing

For a fixed-duration effort above CP, riding at even power maximises the distance covered. Surging burns W′ disproportionately: every watt above CP draws down the battery, and recharge below CP is slower than depletion above it.

### Interval Design

- **To stress CP:** work at or just above CP with short recoveries, so W′ never fully recharges. This is what makes 30/15s and similar formats so potent.
- **To stress W′:** short, very hard efforts (30s-2min at 130-170% of FTP) with long, full recoveries so the battery refills between reps.

### Race Tactics

A rider whose W′ is spent has nothing left for the decisive move regardless of how fresh their aerobic system feels. Budget W′ across a race the way you budget matches: count them, and know how many you have.

## Durability — The Fourth Determinant

Classic endurance physiology names three determinants of performance: VO2max, lactate/anaerobic threshold, and gross efficiency (Joyner & Coyle, 2008). Maunder et al. (2021) argued for a fourth: **durability** — the time of onset and magnitude of deterioration in those characteristics during prolonged exercise.

### Why It Matters

Nearly all laboratory profiling is done fresh. Races are not won fresh. Two riders with the same fresh FTP may differ by 15-20% in the power they can hold after 2,500 kJ of work. In professional racing, the decisive climb almost always comes after several hours of riding — so durability, not fresh threshold, frequently decides the outcome.

### Measuring Durability

- **Drift-based:** compare a submaximal steady effort early in a ride to the same effort late in the ride. Track heart-rate drift, RPE drift, and efficiency changes at matched power.
- **Performance-based:** perform a maximal test (5 min or 20 min) after a fixed amount of accumulated work — commonly 1,500-3,000 kJ. Express the result as a percentage of the same test done fresh.
- **From ride data:** compare best 5-min and 20-min power in the first hour of a ride against best power after 2,000+ kJ, across many rides.

A well-developed rider loses only a small percentage; a poorly durable rider can lose 20% or more.

### Training Durability

- **Long Zone 2 rides** are the foundation. Volume of accumulated work is the primary driver.
- **Intensity late in long rides:** place threshold or VO2max intervals in the final hour of a long endurance ride rather than at the start. This is the most specific durability stimulus available.
- **Fasted or lower-carb-availability Zone 2** (used sparingly, never before key sessions) may support the substrate-metabolism side of durability. Do not apply this to hard sessions.
- **Deliberate under-fuelling in racing is not durability training** — it is a route to underperformance and low energy availability.

## Practical Coaching Rules

- Reassess CP/W′ on the same 6-8 week cadence as FTP.
- If a rider's complaint is "I'm fine for the first two hours and useless after," the problem is durability, not FTP. Prescribe volume and late-ride intensity, not more threshold work when fresh.
- If a rider's complaint is "I can time-trial all day but get dropped every time the pace surges," the problem is W′ and repeatability. Prescribe short high-intensity intervals with incomplete recovery.
- Never diagnose either from a single ride. Both require a pattern across weeks.
