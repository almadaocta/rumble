# Training and Racing in Heat and at Altitude

## Overview

Heat and altitude both degrade performance acutely and both can be adapted to with structured exposure. Heat acclimation is the higher-return, lower-risk intervention for most amateur riders — it is cheap, takes 1-2 weeks, and produces meaningful benefit even for sea-level racing in temperate conditions. Altitude training is logistically expensive and its benefits are smaller and more variable than commonly believed.

## Heat — Why Performance Falls

Exercise in the heat raises core and skin temperature, which drives blood flow to the skin for cooling. That competes directly with blood flow to working muscle. The consequences: elevated heart rate at any given power (cardiovascular drift), higher perceived effort, faster glycogen use, and progressive dehydration if sweat losses are not replaced (Périard et al., 2021).

Endurance performance can fall by **10% or more** in hot conditions relative to temperate, and the deficit grows with duration.

## Heat Acclimation Protocol

The evidence-supported approach (Périard et al., 2021):

- **Duration:** 60-90 minutes per session in hot conditions.
- **Frequency:** consecutive or near-consecutive days.
- **Length of block:** 1-2 weeks. Most of the adaptation appears within 7-10 days; a 5-day block captures a useful fraction.
- **Timing:** begin at least 2 weeks before the target event, finishing before the taper.
- **Intensity:** moderate, mostly Zone 2 with some tempo. The goal is elevated core temperature and sweating, not a hard session. Do not stack heat acclimation onto key intensity sessions.

### Practical Implementation Without a Heat Chamber

- Ride indoors with fans off or minimal, in warm clothing, at moderate intensity.
- Ride outdoors in the hottest part of the day, overdressed.
- **Post-exercise passive heating:** a hot bath or sauna immediately after a normal training session is a well-tolerated alternative that avoids compromising training quality.

### Adaptations to Expect

- **Plasma volume expansion** (an early and substantial adaptation, appearing within days)
- **Earlier sweat onset and higher sweat rate**
- **Reduced sweat sodium concentration** — better electrolyte conservation
- **Lower heart rate and lower core temperature** at a given workload
- **Reduced perceived exertion** in the heat

Adaptations begin to decay within days to weeks of stopping. Maintain with one or two heat sessions per week if the event is still distant.

### Safety Rules

- Hydrate aggressively during heat acclimation sessions (see the nutrition knowledge base on hydration and electrolytes).
- Stop immediately for dizziness, confusion, chills, or cessation of sweating — these are heat-illness signs, not toughness to push through.
- Do not heat-acclimate while ill, sleep-deprived, or in a large energy deficit.
- Older riders, riders on medication affecting thermoregulation, and riders with cardiovascular conditions should get medical clearance first.

## Racing in the Heat — Tactical Adjustments

- **Reduce power targets.** Expect to hold 3-8% less power in significant heat. Racing to a temperate-condition power number is the classic way to blow up.
- **Pace by RPE and heart rate, not by watts alone.**
- **Pre-cool** where practical: cold drinks and ice slurry before the start, ice in a jersey or neck collar.
- **Increase fluid and sodium intake** and start hydrated rather than trying to catch up mid-race.
- **Expect heart-rate drift** — do not interpret it as a fitness problem.

## Altitude — The Physiology

At altitude, reduced barometric pressure lowers the oxygen partial pressure in inspired air, reducing arterial oxygen saturation and therefore VO2max. The practical effect for cyclists: **roughly 6-8% loss of VO2max per 1,000 m above about 1,500 m**, though individual variation is wide. Aerobic efforts suffer most; short sprints are barely affected, and in fact benefit from reduced aerodynamic drag in thinner air.

## Live High, Train Low

The landmark study (Levine & Stray-Gundersen, 1997) randomised 39 competitive runners across four weeks to live-high/train-low (living at 2,500 m, training at 1,250 m), live-high/train-high, or live-low/train-low. The live-high/train-low group improved sea-level performance most, via roughly a 5% increase in red-cell mass combined with the ability to maintain sea-level training intensity.

### Dose Requirements

The commonly cited requirements for a meaningful haematological response:

- **Living altitude:** approximately **2,000-2,500 m**. Below about 1,800 m the hypoxic stimulus is too weak; above roughly 3,000 m sleep, recovery, and training quality degrade.
- **Exposure:** more than **12 hours per day** at altitude.
- **Duration:** minimum **21 days**, preferably 3-4 weeks.
- **Training altitude:** low enough (roughly 1,200-1,500 m or lower) that high-intensity sessions can be executed at sea-level power.

### Why It Often Fails in Practice

- **Insufficient dose.** A one-week training camp at altitude produces no haematological benefit and a lot of poor-quality training.
- **Iron deficiency.** Red-cell mass expansion requires adequate iron stores. Riders with low ferritin will not respond. Screen and correct before any altitude block.
- **Energy deficit.** Appetite is suppressed at altitude while energy expenditure rises. Under-fuelling negates the adaptation.
- **Poor sleep** in the first several nights.
- **Individual non-response** is well documented — a meaningful proportion of athletes simply do not increase red-cell mass.

### Timing the Return

Sea-level performance after an altitude block is not uniformly good on every day. The commonly used windows are the **first 1-3 days after descent** or **roughly days 14-21**, with a dip in between. This is variable enough between individuals that it should be tested in a non-critical block before being relied on for an A event.

## Coaching Guidance — What to Actually Recommend

For the large majority of amateur riders:

1. **Heat acclimation is worth doing.** It is cheap, effective, and low risk. Recommend it before any hot target event.
2. **Altitude training is usually not worth it.** The dose required (3+ weeks living at 2,000-2,500 m) is out of reach for most, and half-measures are counterproductive.
3. **If a rider is racing at altitude,** arrive either immediately before the event (within 24 hours, before acute mountain sickness symptoms peak) or with at least 10-14 days to acclimatise. Arriving 3-5 days out is the worst option.
4. **Adjust power expectations at altitude** and re-derive interval targets rather than trying to hit sea-level numbers.
