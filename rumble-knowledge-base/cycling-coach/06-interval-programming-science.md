# Interval Programming Science — Designing HIIT That Works

## Overview

"Do intervals" is not a prescription. Buchheit and Laursen (2013) framed HIIT design as a puzzle with nine variables, each of which shifts *which* physiological system is stressed. This document covers how to manipulate those variables deliberately, and what the evidence says about short versus long VO2max intervals.

## The Nine Programming Variables

Every interval session is defined by (Buchheit & Laursen, 2013, Part I and Part II):

1. **Work interval intensity** — the primary driver of which system is taxed.
2. **Work interval duration** — determines how long the target intensity can be held.
3. **Recovery interval intensity** — active vs passive changes reoxygenation and lactate clearance.
4. **Recovery interval duration** — governs how completely W′ recharges between efforts.
5. **Exercise modality** — cycling loads the legs differently from running; interference and soreness differ.
6. **Number of repetitions** — per set.
7. **Number of sets** — sets allow longer recoveries and more total quality work.
8. **Between-set recovery duration** — the lever for extending a session without losing intensity.
9. **Between-set recovery intensity** — same logic as within-set recovery.

Changing any one variable changes the session's target. The practical implication for coaching: **name the physiological target first, then choose the variables** — never the reverse.

## Time Above 90% VO2max — The Key Currency

For sessions targeting VO2max, the accepted proxy for adaptive stimulus is **accumulated time above 90% of VO2max**. Oxygen uptake takes 60-120 seconds to rise into that zone, so:

- Very short efforts with full recovery never accumulate meaningful time above 90%.
- Very long efforts at maximal sustainable intensity accumulate time but force a lower power output and cause disproportionate fatigue.
- The winning formats sit in between, or use short work bouts with *short* recoveries so oxygen uptake never falls back down.

## Short Intervals vs Long Intervals

Rønnestad and colleagues (2020) compared two formats in elite cyclists over 3 weeks, matched for perceived effort:

- **Short-interval group:** 3 sets of 13 × 30 seconds hard / 15 seconds easy, with 3 minutes between sets (the "30/15" format).
- **Long-interval group:** 4 × 5 minutes with 2.5 minutes recovery.

The short-interval group improved more: gains in VO2max, power at approximately lactate threshold, and mean power in 5-minute and 40-minute all-out tests. The long-interval group did not show significant improvement in 5-minute power over the same period.

### Why Short Intervals Work

During the 15-second easy segments, oxygen uptake barely falls. The rider stays in the high-VO2 zone across the whole set while the muscles get repeated micro-recoveries — so peripheral fatigue accumulates more slowly than in a continuous 5-minute effort at the same average oxygen cost. The result is more time above 90% VO2max for less perceived misery.

### Caveats — Don't Over-Apply This

- One 3-week study in already-elite cyclists is not proof that short intervals beat long intervals for everyone, forever.
- **Novelty matters.** Riders who have done nothing but 4 × 5 min for two years respond to 30/15s partly because it is new. The reverse is equally true.
- Long intervals remain more specific for riders whose events demand sustained 5-20 minute climbing power.
- **Rotate formats across a season.** Do not build an entire year on one interval shape.

## Format Selection by Target

### VO2max (Zone 5)
- **30/15s:** 3 sets × 13 reps, 30s at ~110-120% FTP / 15s at ~50% FTP, 3 min between sets.
- **40/20s:** 3 sets × 10-13 reps, same structure, slightly longer work bouts.
- **4 × 4-5 min:** at 105-115% FTP, 2.5-4 min recovery. The classic. Most specific to sustained climbing.
- **Ronnestad-style descending sets:** useful when the rider fades badly across sets.

### Anaerobic Capacity / W′ (Zone 6)
- 6-10 × 1 min at 130-150% FTP with 2-4 min *full* recovery.
- The long recovery is the point — W′ must recharge or the session becomes a poorly executed VO2max session.

### Threshold (Zone 4)
- 2 × 20 min, 3 × 15 min, or 4 × 10 min at 95-105% FTP, 5 min recovery.
- **Over-unders:** alternating 2 min at 105% / 2 min at 90% within a longer block, to train lactate clearance while still producing it.

### Repeatability / Race Specificity
- Micro-bursts inside a tempo block: 15s at 150% FTP every minute while holding ~75% FTP between bursts.
- Directly trains the surge-recover-surge demand of road racing.

## Recovery Interval Intensity — An Underused Lever

- **Active recovery (40-50% FTP):** keeps oxygen uptake elevated, increases time above 90% VO2max, improves lactate clearance. Default for VO2max work.
- **Passive recovery (soft-pedal or stop):** allows faster phosphocreatine and W′ restoration, permitting higher power in the next rep. Default for anaerobic and neuromuscular work.

## Session Placement and Frequency

- **Two high-intensity sessions per week** is the sustainable ceiling for most amateur riders training 8-12 hours. Three is possible in a short block, not as a steady state.
- Separate hard sessions by at least 48 hours where possible.
- Never schedule a VO2max session the day after a heavy lower-body gym session (see the strength and conditioning knowledge base on concurrent-training fatigue).
- Full carbohydrate availability is required. A VO2max session executed on depleted glycogen produces a worse stimulus at a higher cost.

## Judging Whether a Session Worked

A well-executed interval session should show:
- **Consistent or rising power across reps** — a large drop-off after the first set means the prescription was too hard.
- **Final rep completable** — if the rider fails the last rep of the last set, the session was mildly over-prescribed; if they fail in set one, it was badly over-prescribed.
- **Heart rate reaching 90%+ of max** during the later reps of VO2max work.

If a rider completes every rep comfortably with power to spare, the intensity was too low and the session was tempo work in disguise.
