# Concurrent Training and the Interference Effect

## Overview

"Concurrent training" means training strength and endurance in the same programme. The "interference effect" is the observation that doing both blunts the gains from one or both compared with doing either alone. For cyclists this is mostly good news — the interference is smaller than the folklore suggests, and cycling is the *least* interfering endurance modality. But the scheduling implications are real, and they run in the opposite direction from what most riders assume.

## What the Meta-Analysis Found

Wilson et al. (2012) meta-analysed 21 studies with 422 effect sizes, comparing strength training alone, endurance training alone, and concurrent training.

**Hypertrophy effect sizes:** strength training 1.23, concurrent training 0.85, endurance training 0.27.
**Strength effect sizes:** strength training 1.76, concurrent training 1.44, endurance training 0.78.

Concurrent training therefore produced *most* of the strength and hypertrophy of strength training alone — a real but moderate interference, not an abolition.

### The Finding That Matters Most for Cyclists

**Interference was significant when resistance training was combined with running, but not with cycling.** The mechanical similarity and lower eccentric loading of cycling make it far less disruptive to strength adaptation than running.

Effect sizes also correlated negatively with endurance training **frequency** (−0.26 to −0.35) and **duration** (−0.29 to −0.75) — the more and longer the endurance work, the greater the interference. Duration was the stronger of the two.

### What This Means in Practice

A cyclist doing two gym sessions per week alongside a normal riding load can expect to gain strength effectively. A rider doing 20+ hours of riding a week will see meaningfully blunted strength gains — and should accept that, because for them the riding is the point.

## The Molecular Story — And Why Not to Over-Read It

The classic mechanistic explanation is that endurance exercise activates AMPK, which inhibits mTOR signalling and therefore suppresses the protein synthesis that drives hypertrophy.

Coffey and Hawley (2017) reviewed this and concluded the picture is considerably less tidy than the AMPK-mTOR "switch" model implies. Chronic training studies robustly show endurance exercise can attenuate hypertrophy and strength, but the mechanistic underpinning of the interference effect **remains unresolved**.

**Coaching implication:** do not make programme decisions from molecular reasoning. The chronic-training evidence — which sessions, in which order, with how much recovery — is what should drive scheduling.

## The Other Direction — Resistance Training Impairing Endurance

The interference conversation usually focuses on endurance harming strength. Doma et al. (2017) argued the reverse direction is the more practically important one for endurance athletes.

A single bout of resistance training produces residual fatigue that can impair subsequent endurance training quality **for several hours to days** when recovery is inadequate. The proposed mechanisms:

1. Impaired neural recruitment patterns
2. Reduced movement efficiency — altered kinematics and increased energy expenditure at a given output
3. Increased muscle soreness
4. Reduced muscle glycogen

**The consequence:** if a rider's key endurance sessions are consistently executed on legs still carrying gym fatigue, the endurance development itself is compromised. The rider loses on both sides — not from a molecular interference effect, but from simply training badly.

This is why session scheduling matters more than session content for most riders.

## Scheduling Principles

Detailed timing guidance is in the session timing document. The principles that follow from the interference literature:

### Priority Determines Order

- **Strength is the priority (off-season):** put the gym session first, when fresh. Accept temporarily reduced ride quality.
- **Cycling is the priority (in-season — the normal case):** the key ride comes first. Gym work goes after the ride on the same day, or on an easy/rest day.

### The Same-Day Rule

**If both must happen on one day, do them in the same session block or separated by at least 6 hours.** The worst arrangement is a gym session 2-4 hours before a hard ride — the rider carries acute fatigue into the quality work with no time to clear it.

**Hard ride in the morning, gym in the afternoon** is the standard in-season arrangement. It concentrates fatigue into one day and leaves the following day cleaner.

### Never Schedule

- A heavy lower-body gym session the day **before** a key VO2max or threshold session
- Maximal strength work within 48 hours of a race
- A new or unfamiliar lower-body exercise during a hard training block — unfamiliar eccentric loading is the fastest route to soreness that ruins the following week

### Weekly Structure

- **Off-season / early base:** 2-3 gym sessions per week, on lower-intensity riding days.
- **Build:** 2 sessions per week, reduced volume, placed after key rides.
- **In-season:** 1-2 maintenance sessions per week (see the strength maintenance document).
- **Race week:** at most one light session early in the week, or none.

## Fuelling the Interference Away

Part of the interference effect is not molecular at all — it is under-recovery. Riders doing both should:

- Take adequate carbohydrate to support both training modes; glycogen depletion worsens both interference and gym-session quality
- Take **1.6-2.2 g/kg/day of protein**, distributed across meals, with 0.3 g/kg post-session
- Prioritise sleep during concurrent blocks — the recovery demand is higher than either mode alone

See the nutrition knowledge base for detail.

## The Bottom Line for Cyclists

1. **Interference is real but modest, and cycling interferes less than running.** Do not let fear of interference stop a cyclist from strength training — the performance evidence for strength work in cyclists is strong (Rønnestad & Mujika, 2014).
2. **The bigger practical risk is gym fatigue degrading key rides**, not endurance work degrading strength.
3. **Schedule around that risk.** Hard days hard, easy days easy, gym attached to hard days or easy days — never sandwiched before quality work.
4. **Endurance duration is the strongest interference variable.** A rider adding a lot of long-ride volume should expect strength progression to slow and should shift the gym to maintenance rather than fighting it.
