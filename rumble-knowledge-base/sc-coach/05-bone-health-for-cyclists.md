# Bone Health for Cyclists

## Overview

Cycling is one of the few endurance sports that is actively bad for bone. It is non-weight-bearing, produces almost no impact loading, and involves large calcium losses through sweat on long rides — combined, in many riders, with chronic energy deficit. The result is a well-documented pattern of low bone mineral density in dedicated road cyclists.

This is the strongest health-based argument for strength training in cyclists, independent of any performance benefit. A rider who does not care about gaining watts in the gym should still care about this.

## What the Evidence Shows

Olmedillas et al. (2012) systematically reviewed 31 studies of cycling and bone health. The findings:

- **Adult road cyclists who train regularly have significantly lower bone mineral density** than both athletes in other sports and non-athletes, at the **lumbar spine, pelvic and hip regions, and femoral neck**.
- The effect scales with training exposure. Amateur and low-level cyclists generally showed no significant difference from controls, whereas **elite cyclists and those training hard for many years consistently showed low bone mineral density**.
- Training hard over long distances for multiple years therefore increases the risk of osteopenia and osteoporosis, and with it, fracture risk.

**Mountain bikers appear less affected** than road cyclists — the vibration and impact of riding uneven terrain plausibly provides some protective loading that smooth-road riding does not.

### Why This Is Particularly Dangerous in Cycling

Cyclists crash. A sport that systematically lowers bone density while regularly throwing riders onto tarmac at speed is a bad combination — and it explains why hip and proximal femoral fractures show up in cyclist populations at ages where they would be unexpected.

## Contributing Factors

1. **No weight-bearing or impact loading.** Bone remodels in response to mechanical strain, particularly high-magnitude, high-rate, multidirectional strain. Pedalling provides almost none of it.
2. **Dermal calcium loss.** Substantial calcium is lost in sweat during long rides, and low blood calcium promotes bone resorption to restore it.
3. **Low energy availability.** Impairs bone formation directly and disrupts the hormonal environment (see the nutrition knowledge base on energy availability and REDs). This is likely the single largest modifiable factor for many riders.
4. **Menstrual dysfunction** in female riders, itself usually driven by low energy availability.
5. **Suppressed testosterone** in male riders under chronic energy deficit.
6. **Prolonged sedentary time off the bike** — many riders train hard and then sit for the rest of the day.

## The Loading Intervention

Bone responds to **high-magnitude, rapidly applied, varied-direction loading**. Duration and repetition matter far less than intensity and novelty of strain — bone adapts quickly to a repeated stimulus and stops responding to it.

### Heavy Resistance Training

The most reliable intervention available, and it doubles as performance work:

- **Squats, deadlifts, and their variations** — axial loading through the spine and hips, exactly where cyclists lose density.
- **Heavy loads matter.** 3-6 repetitions at 80-90% of 1RM produces greater osteogenic strain than higher-repetition, lighter work.
- **2-3 sessions per week** during off-season and early base; maintained through the season.
- Progressive overload is the mechanism. A rider lifting the same weights for two years is no longer providing a novel stimulus.

See the exercise database and session templates documents for programming detail.

### Impact and Plyometric Loading

- **Jumping and hopping** — box jumps, drop jumps, bounding, skipping.
- **Short and frequent beats long and occasional.** Roughly **10-20 jumps, 2-3 times per week** captures most of the available benefit; bone desensitises to repeated loading within a session, so more jumps in one go adds little.
- **Multidirectional loading** — vary direction and landing pattern rather than repeating identical vertical jumps.
- **Progress carefully.** A cyclist with low bone density and untrained tendons is exactly the person who gets injured jumping off a box on day one. Start with low-amplitude skipping and hopping on both feet, build over weeks.

### Complementary Activities

- **Running or hiking**, even in small amounts, provides impact loading cycling does not. A weekly hike or a short easy jog in the off-season is useful — introduce it gradually given cyclists' poor tolerance for eccentric loading.
- **Off-season sports involving jumping and changes of direction.**
- **Simply standing more** during the day.

## The Nutrition Side

Loading without adequate substrate and energy does not build bone.

- **Calcium:** particularly important given dermal losses. Long, hot rides increase the requirement. Some evidence supports taking calcium *before* long rides to blunt the resorption response, though the practice is not firmly established.
- **Vitamin D:** required for calcium absorption. Insufficiency is common in northern latitudes and in winter indoor-training riders. Test and correct.
- **Energy availability:** the foundation. **No amount of loading will build bone in a rider who is chronically under-fuelled.** If a rider has low bone density and low energy availability, correcting the energy deficit takes priority over adding squats.
- **Protein:** adequate intake supports the bone matrix, not just muscle.

Refer to a registered dietitian for individual assessment and to a physician for any supplementation decisions.

## Screening and Escalation

**Recommend a DEXA scan and medical review for riders who:**
- Have trained as dedicated road cyclists for many years with little or no resistance training
- Have sustained a stress fracture or a fracture from a low-energy fall
- Have a history of menstrual dysfunction or amenorrhoea
- Have known or suspected low energy availability or a history of disordered eating
- Are masters-age riders with a long cycling history, especially post-menopausal women

**Bone density interpretation and any pharmacological treatment is a physician's decision.** A strength and conditioning specialist's role is to identify risk, recommend screening, and programme the loading intervention.

## The Message to Give Riders

Most cyclists resist gym work because they doubt it will make them faster, and the performance case — while real — is arguable. **The bone case is not arguable.** Framed correctly, it changes minds:

> Cycling makes your bones weaker every year you do it. You also fall off at 40 km/h several times a career. Two heavy lifting sessions a week is what stops those two facts from meeting badly.

For masters riders in particular, this is the most important thing in the strength and conditioning knowledge base.
